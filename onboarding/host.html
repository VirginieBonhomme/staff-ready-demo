Host — Your First Week

Role: Host New Hire Path Updated: May 2026

This is your path through the first week as a host. It is not a general orientation — it is a day-by-day reading list specific to this role that tells you exactly what to read, in what order, before you own the door alone. Follow it in sequence. Each SOP takes a few minutes to read and will make the difference between a smooth service and a chaotic one.

At the end of the week, you sign off to confirm you have read and understood everything. That sign-off goes to your manager. It is not a test. It is a record that you were given the information.

Before Your First Shift

Three reads. Fifteen minutes. Do this before you arrive.

These three SOPs are the non-negotiable starting points for the host role. They cover hygiene, allergen communication, and the greeting and seating sequence. The host is the first person every guest interacts with — and the handoff you make at the table carries that guest's allergy information into the kitchen. Read these before your first shift. You will be expected to know them.

Read in order 01 Handwashing Procedure ~2 min 02 Allergen Communication ~4 min 03 Greeting & Seating ~4 min

By the time you arrive for your first shift, you should know the correct greeting sequence, understand why the allergy handoff at the table is your responsibility — not the guest's — and be able to describe what happens when a guest with an allergy is seated.

Day One

Shadow the door. Watch the flow. Do not seat a table alone.

Your first shift is about orientation, not ownership. You will shadow your trainer through the full service — from opening the entrance and checking the room, through the service rush, to closing the host stand. Watch how reservations are read before service, how the seating plan is managed under pressure, and how the team communicates. You are not expected to work independently. You are expected to be attentive and ask questions.

Confirm each of these with your trainer before service starts:

How to open the reservations system and where today's bookings are displayed
How to read the seating chart and understand which sections belong to which server
Where allergy notes appear in the reservation system and how they are communicated at handoff
How to walk a guest to a table — not point and direct
What to say when quoting a walk-in wait time
Where fire exits, restrooms, and accessibility routes are
Who is shift lead today and when to escalate

At the end of day one, you should know the room layout, how the reservation system works at a basic level, and what the correct allergy handoff looks like at the table. You do not need to be fast. You need to know the process.

Day Two

Learn how the booking system and guest recovery work.

Before your second shift, read these two SOPs. They cover reservations and waitlist management, and complaints and recovery. On day two you will begin to assist with seating guests under supervision. You are not running the door independently yet, but you will be expected to know what correct looks like in both the smooth moments and the difficult ones.

Read before this shift 04 Reservations & Waitlist ~4 min 05 Complaints & Recovery ~4 min 06 Host Checklist ~3 min

By the end of day two, you should understand how reservations are managed when a party is late, how walk-in waits are communicated accurately, and what to do when a guest at the door is becoming agitated. Your trainer will walk you through managing the waitlist during service.

Day Three Onward

The operational routines that hold the floor together.

By day three you should be assisting with seating and preparing to run the door with light supervision. These reads cover the full FOH opening checklist and the pre-service brief — the two routines that set up every service before the first guest arrives. A host who understands both is genuinely useful to the whole front-of-house team.

Read before day three 07 FOH Opening Checklist ~3 min 08 FOH Closing Checklist ~3 min 09 Pre-Service Brief ~2 min

You are expected to have the entrance, host stand, menus, and waiting area ready before the pre-service brief starts. By day four you should be running those tasks independently without being prompted.

What Is Expected of You

Standards that apply from day one, not from week two.

These are not things you will be forgiven for not knowing once you have read this page.

Allergy handoff When you seat a guest with an allergy, you tell the server at the table — clearly, by name, while handing off. You do not rely on the guest to repeat it themselves. The server confirms it back to you before you leave. This handoff is your responsibility and it happens every time, no exceptions.
30-second greeting Every guest who walks through the door is acknowledged within 30 seconds. Not greeted and seated — acknowledged. If you are busy with another table or a call, you make eye contact and signal that you have seen them. No guest stands at the entrance wondering if anyone knows they are there.
Section pacing You do not seat three tables in the same section back to back during a rush unless the manager says so. Pacing protects the kitchen and the server. A section flooded all at once leads to slower food, overwhelmed staff, and unhappy guests. When in doubt, ask the shift lead before seating.
Accurate wait times You never underquote a wait time to keep a guest happy. A guest told 15 minutes and seated in 25 is more forgiving than a guest told 10 minutes and seated in 30. Quote what is realistic, check back if it is running long, and never leave a waiting guest without an update.
Asking for help A new host who asks questions is doing the job right. Ask your trainer or shift lead before you improvise, especially with allergies, VIPs, accessibility needs, or complaints. These are not situations to figure out on your own in week one.
End of First Week

Sign off to confirm you have read and understood this material.

This form asks you to confirm, by name, that you have read all nine SOPs and checklists listed above and that you understand the standards on this page. It takes two minutes. It is timestamped and goes to your manager automatically.

If there is anything on this page you are not clear on before you submit, go back and reread it, or ask your manager before you sign. Once submitted, this is your record that you were trained.

Complete First Week Sign-Off

Submissions are logged in the manager's onboarding sheet. If you cannot access the form, ask your manager to log it manually before the end of your first week.
